# Simple Learner: goals, results, academic critique and next experiments

**Date:** 11 November 2026  
**Project:** [pilgrimbeart/simple-learner](https://github.com/pilgrimbeart/simple-learner)  
**Reviewed commit:** [d96b2152ef50a897069bc4fa6c6d6bd32768d958](https://github.com/pilgrimbeart/simple-learner/tree/d96b2152ef50a897069bc4fa6c6d6bd32768d958)

**The project has produced useful evidence for learning from play, and promising examples of capacity growing with a task. It has not yet demonstrated its central ambition: a general, locally organised mechanism that turns additional material into additional intelligence.** The most valuable next experiment would test whether the successful play mechanism can discover how its knowledge should control behaviour, without the motor-balancing rule currently supplied by the designer.

I reviewed commit `d96b215`, including the goals, experimental notebooks, relevant implementations and saved results through D48. I have not independently rerun the simulations. The critique below uses MacKay’s characteristic approach—explicit assumptions, information accounting and discriminating experiments—without attributing imagined opinions to him.

Your goal is sufficiently clear that I don’t need clarification.

The ambition is more demanding than “build a small learner”. It combines:

- **Learning during life**, including acquiring useful structure before reward arrives.
- **Adaptation followed by recruitment**, with generic components allocated where needed.
- **Scaling without redesign**, across different kinds of challenge.
- **Local operation in a noisy, embodied, real-time system**, with material, communication and energy costs eventually counted.

That conjunction is the interesting research question. Demonstrating any one element alone would leave substantial work.

The results so far deserve different levels of confidence:

| Line of work | What the evidence supports | What remains open |
|---|---|---|
| Small circuits | Tiny controllers can forage and learn the basic food/poison association in transistor-level simulation. | This establishes feasibility for those controllers; it does not establish physical feasibility of the newer learning machinery. |
| Bank of predictors | On 61 random worlds classified as having something to learn, 11 definitely passed, 24 definitely failed and 26 remained unresolved. Missing temporal hypotheses explain important failures. | Generality beyond the supplied repertoire and development-world distribution. |
| Registers and growing trees | Additional material supports longer sequences and more complex combinations. The tree reaches sequence rung 17 and combination rung 6; timing remains weaker. | The complete scaling test, including three clean doublings across ladders, locality, costs and the held-out ladder. |
| Play before reward | On fresh seeds, the motor-record mechanism improves the first 100 seconds after reward begins by **1.51 net meals per 10 seconds**, with reported 95% interval **[0.59, 2.43]**. | Whether the useful measurement-to-control relationship can itself be learned. |
| “Boredom from certainty” | D48 reports encouraging accuracy and later performance. | It is explicitly exploratory, developed on those seeds, and its confidence score needs calibration. |

These distinctions are largely present in the [notebook](https://github.com/pilgrimbeart/simple-learner/blob/d96b2152ef50a897069bc4fa6c6d6bd32768d958/discrete/NOTEBOOK.md). Some qualifications get lost in the shorter summaries.

Several findings are particularly valuable: more random hypotheses bought surprisingly little; prediction regularity selected the wrong movements; an unchanged estimate could be confidently wrong; and an allegedly uninformative movement became informative when the observer was improved. Those failures constrain the next design.

My main criticisms are these.

1. **The information accounting is useful, but some conclusions are stronger than the accounting allows.**

   “A life supplies about 50 bits, whereas describing a world takes hundreds” does **not** establish that the creature cannot learn what it needs during that life.

   Three quantities are being mixed:

   - The length of our description of the world.
   - The uncertainty the animal must resolve to act well.
   - The information its observations supply about that uncertainty.

   A world can require a long program to simulate while the useful decision depends on one bit. Conversely, a short rule can be difficult to identify because the animal rarely encounters distinguishing evidence.

   The appropriate quantity is something like

   $$
   I(\Theta;O_{1:T}\mid \pi,\text{prior}),
   $$

   where $\Theta$ is the relevant unknown, $O$ includes the available sensory observations and their timing, and $\pi$ specifies how the animal gathers them.

   Your D31 calculation of roughly $n[1-H_2(e)]$ is informative under its binary-channel assumptions. It is not a universal budget for the whole sensory stream. D31 itself recognises several of these limitations.

   Likewise, $\log_2 K$ is the identification cost for **equiprobable, distinguishable** alternatives. It is not a guarantee that an arbitrary collection of $K$ predictors can be identified after that much experience.

   The stronger formulation is: **learning is fast when useful distinctions are cheap under the learner’s prior and its actions expose those distinctions.** Your play experiments illustrate precisely this.

2. **The latest success contains a substantial piece of your understanding of the body.**

   In [play3.py](https://github.com/pilgrimbeart/simple-learner/blob/d96b2152ef50a897069bc4fa6c6d6bd32768d958/lab/play3.py), the successful route:

   - Selects stretches when one motor acts alone.
   - Estimates recurrence periods from motor-clocked sensory records.
   - Takes the difference between the logarithms of the two periods.
   - Sets reciprocal motor gains from that difference.

   That is a good system-identification solution. It also encodes the relationship between recurrence, actuator strength and balanced propulsion.

   Consequently, D47 demonstrates that **unrewarded experience can estimate useful body parameters and improve subsequent performance through a designed control relationship**. It does not yet demonstrate discovery of that relationship.

   This matters because the programme explicitly seeks a generic link “with no formula of ours”. Calling the link “partners” makes its assumption easier to understand; it does not remove the assumption.

   There is no need to eliminate all inherited structure—every learner has some. The task is to state what is inherited and show that it transfers beyond the situation that suggested it.

3. **Predictability, learning progress and useful information are different objectives.**

   Your D45 result is an excellent demonstration. A movement can make sensations highly predictable while teaching little about the distinction needed later.

   There are four different questions:

   | Objective | What it asks |
   |---|---|
   | Predictability | Can I predict the next observation? |
   | Surprise | Did this observation violate my prediction? |
   | Information gain | Did it distinguish plausible explanations? |
   | Decision value | Will that distinction improve a consequential choice? |

   MacKay’s own 1992 paper on active data selection distinguished objectives according to **what we want information about**, and explicitly warned that their usefulness depends on the hypothesis space being appropriate. That paper is directly relevant here. [Source: MacKay, 1992](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/efefp-2j353).

   Recent work reinforces the distinction. *Beyond Noisy-TVs* at ICLR 2026 measures improvements in a dynamics model rather than simply rewarding prediction error; it also uses machinery to separate improvement from expected error. Your noisy difference between successive short play turns is a much rougher estimator. Its failure does not establish that learning progress is the wrong principle. [Source: Hou, An and Du, 2026](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/803b9c4a8e4784072fdd791c54d614e2-Abstract-Conference.html).

   Similarly, **a larger confidence margin is not automatically better knowledge**. In D48, the margin is between peaks of a recurrence score. It is not a posterior probability or a calibrated error bound. Correlated lags, searching over peaks and repeated stopping checks all matter.

   The obvious test is empirical: when the mechanism says it is certain, how often is it wrong—especially about doubled and halved periods?

4. **The project is converging on established ideas; its opportunity lies in their implementation and integration.**

   Learning from unrewarded interaction and transferring that knowledge to later tasks is well established. Plan2Explore explicitly learns through exploration before downstream rewards are supplied. Martius, Der and Ay demonstrated information-driven organisation of embodied behaviour, including decentralised control. These are close conceptual relatives, not merely background inspiration. Sources: [Sekar et al., 2020](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/sekar20a.html); [Martius, Der and Ay, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3664628/).

   Active identification of a robot’s dynamics remains current research: SPI-Active, presented at CoRL 2025, deliberately selects commands that make physical parameters easier to identify. Its methods do not meet your locality constraints, but it provides a useful performance reference. [Source: Sobanbabu et al., 2025](https://lecar-lab.github.io/spi-active_/).

   Your distinctive opportunity is therefore quite specific:

   **Can compact, local, reusable mechanisms acquire predictive structure through action, use sparse rewards to select useful behaviour, and expand their capacity without external allocation?**

   That is a strong question even if its ingredients have names already.

5. **The growing tree is promising, but it is not yet the Bayesian machinery invoked to motivate it.**

   The implementation in [tree.py](https://github.com/pilgrimbeart/simple-learner/blob/d96b2152ef50a897069bc4fa6c6d6bd32768d958/lab/tree.py) grows after contradictory observations and predicts from the deepest suitably populated context. Context-tree weighting instead recursively combines predictions across alternative tree structures, with explicit probabilistic coding guarantees. Sharing a tree structure does not transfer those guarantees. [Source: Willems, Shtarkov and Tjalkens, 1995](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/the-context-tree-weighting-method-basic-properties/).

   This suggests a useful comparison: your current growing tree against genuine context-tree weighting, using identical sensory histories. It would distinguish losses caused by limited capacity from losses caused by how evidence is combined.

   There is also an unavoidable boundary: an arbitrary Boolean mapping over $k$ binary inputs has $2^k$ independently specifiable outputs. No recruitment rule can generally compress that into a few components. To demonstrate compositional intelligence, include challenges whose complexity grows through **reusable structure**, alongside random mappings that deliberately resist compression.

6. **The scaling claim needs an accounting system that grows with the implementation.**

   A “node” currently comes with more than one bit: counters, addressing, history access and prediction logic. The tree has a fixed context-depth limit; the play implementation retains event histories and rescans them; action selection compares candidates centrally.

   These are reasonable exploratory implementations. They leave locality and resource scaling unestablished.

   I would track at least:

   $$
   \text{performance}=f(\text{stored state},\text{experience},\text{computation},\text{communication}).
   $$

   More material and longer lives must be varied separately. Your notebook already identifies the problem of harder rungs sometimes receiving substantially more learning time.

   The latest play metric also measures post-onset food benefit rather than total lifetime energy return. That is appropriate for demonstrating a head start. It cannot yet show that play repays its full energetic cost.

7. **“Adjust first, recruit second” is a good default, not a universal law.**

   Adjusting an existing parameter saves material, but can cost exploration time or destroy a previously useful skill. Sometimes a protected new component is cheaper overall.

   Also distinguish **forgetting old knowledge** from **losing the capacity to learn new knowledge**. Dohare and colleagues’ 2024 continual-learning experiments showed the latter and found that continually replacing some low-utility units could preserve plasticity. This makes recycling a necessary comparator to indefinite growth. [Source: Dohare et al., 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07711-7).

   Recent Generalized Latent Equilibrium work is relevant to your physical ambitions: it derives local, continuous-time learning dynamics that approximate temporal credit assignment. It is a candidate source of mechanisms, rather than evidence that biology or your particular architecture already solves the problem. [Source: Ellenberger et al., published December 2025, 2026 volume](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12770517/).

I would try the following, in order.

1. **First, make the current play result explain itself.**

   Freeze D48 and run one modest fresh comparison, with four arms: no play; uniform random play; learning-progress play; certainty-directed play. Give them equal pre-challenge time and report motor expenditure.

   Measure three things separately: body-estimation error, confidence calibration and later food gain. Include an ablation that gives each selector the same accumulated observations, so you can distinguish better data collection from a better estimator.

   The decisive question is: **does certainty-directed play gather better evidence per unit time or energy, or does it mainly benefit from playing longer?**

   This is worth settling before adding another mechanism. The current results suggest progress, but do not isolate its cause.

2. **Then remove the designed measurement-to-balance formula. This is my highest-priority substantive experiment.**

   Keep the existing body and interface. During play, learn a small **action-conditioned predictor**: given recent sensory history and proposed motor activity, what sensory events should follow? Preserve several plausible predictions where the evidence is ambiguous.

   When taste becomes available, use it to learn which predicted consequences are useful. Start with a conventional forward-model-and-control implementation as an explicitly privileged algorithmic reference; then ask how much of it can be implemented with your local components.

   Compare it with the existing partners rule and taste-only search. Hold the experience budget constant.

   The test is whether unrewarded experience still reduces the subsequent reward-learning burden **without converting recurrence periods to motor gains using the known physical relationship**.

   If it succeeds, the result connects sensory learning to action through a reusable mechanism. If it fails, distinguish whether the predictor lacks information, the reward readout cannot exploit it, or the action search is inadequate.

3. **Make exploration distinguish hypotheses, rather than simply increase confidence.**

   For a reference learner, select actions by expected information gain:

   $$
   a^*=\arg\max_a
   \left[
   I(M;O_{\text{future}}\mid h,a)-\lambda\,C(a)
   \right],
   $$

   where $M$ denotes competing learned models, $h$ is the observed history and $C$ charges time or energy.

   Your period-aliasing failures provide a concrete test: can the learner choose an action whose predicted observations differ between the leading explanations?

   If the explanations predict identical available observations, further play cannot settle the issue through those observations. The learner should retain the ambiguity, seek another source of evidence or let reward discriminate—as your taste-selection result already begins to do.

   Once that reference works, approximate it through local competition between predictive units. This makes the cost of locality measurable.

4. **Finally, reconnect play to recruitment using one shared test.**

   Give a predictive unit three possibilities: keep adjusting, recruit a child that explains a persistent residual, or retire an unhelpful child. Judge new capacity by improvement on **subsequent predictions**, not by its fit to the observations that triggered its creation.

   Compare adaptive recruitment against a fixed small model, a fixed large model and a fixed-size model that recycles weak units. Measure retention and renewed learning after changes, as well as initial success.

   Preserve your lightweight exploratory approach. Five paired lives are useful for detecting large effects and inspecting mechanisms; they are not a general criterion for whether a principle matters. A few frozen, discriminating comparisons will teach more than many increasingly elaborate variants.

The experiment I would start building is therefore: **play learns action-conditioned sensory predictions; a small amount of taste learns how to use them; the physical balancing formula is absent.** Its outcome would address the most consequential gap between the project’s current success and its stated goal.
